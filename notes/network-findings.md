# Network findings (for Turn 13)

What we found when training the network, with the evidence. All numbers are from Year N, cleaned with `training/clean.py`, using only the dark or icy hours (389 of 744) unless stated otherwise. Models were compared with 7-fold cross-validation, split by whole days.

## The result in one sentence

With this data, no input predicts the number of near-accidents better than always guessing the average, so the app sets 40 km/h in every dark or icy hour.

## Why the app always sets 40 km/h

Our rule (04 Round 3, SPEC3b, our own design, not the case study's) picks the highest limit where the network predicts at most 1 near-accident per hour, otherwise 40 km/h. The network predicts 1.32 to 1.45 for every limit in every dark or icy hour, so no limit qualifies.

No limit reaches the target in the data either:

| Limit | Hours | Near-accidents per hour |
|---|---|---|
| 40 | 7 | 1.86 |
| 50 | 55 | 1.44 |
| 60 | 230 | 1.26 |
| 70 | 97 | 1.13 |
| 80 | 0 | never seen in a dark or icy hour |

## Evidence

**Near-accidents happen at the same rate everywhere.** 1.26 per hour in dark or icy hours, 1.33 in all other hours (mostly 80 km/h in daylight).

**The counts behave like pure chance.** Mean 1.26, variance 1.27. For events at a steady random rate, variance equals mean. Single hours range from 0 to 5.

**No model beats the average:**

| Model | Inputs | Mean absolute error |
|---|---|---|
| Always predict the average | none | 0.915 |
| Our MLP | light, water, temperature, limit | 0.916 |
| Poisson regression | same 4 | 0.914 |
| Gradient boosting (Poisson loss) | same 4 | 0.949 |
| Our MLP | + humidity, traffic, hour of day, previous hour's count | 0.973 |

The trained model in `models/` reaches 0.859 on days 25 to 31, against 0.875 for the average (`models/metrics.json`). Always predicting 1 does even better: 0.793, because 1 is the median and the median gives the lowest absolute error (Cristian's 03, NFR12).

**No input correlates with the count by more than ±0.1** (light +0.06, water +0.05, temperature −0.01, humidity −0.02, traffic −0.01, limit −0.10).

**The limit's effect points the wrong way.** Each extra 10 km/h goes with about 12% fewer near-accidents (95% range 3% to 22%, bootstrapped by day). The conditions don't explain it: the limit hardly correlates with light (+0.02), water (−0.03) or temperature (−0.13).

**Severity points the right way, but weakly.** At higher limits cars come closer: 15.8 cm on average at 40 km/h, 14.1 cm at 70. A danger index per hour (closer passes, steeper skids and accidents weigh more, idea from case study slide 17) still can't be predicted: a linear model scored 0.478, against 0.467 for the average.

**Icy hours are the only hint of a real effect,** from only 62 hours:

| Hours | Car too close | Guardrail | Skid | Accident |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dark, not icy (327) | 1.15 | 0.07 | 0.02 | 0.02 |
| Icy (62) | 1.23 | 0.15 | 0.02 | 0.10 |
| Neither (355) | 1.23 | 0.08 | 0.02 | 0.05 |

## The definition of "too close" decides the outcome

The case study (SPEC4) doesn't say what "too close" means. We used 20 cm or closer, because every record is within 20 cm. Skids always count.

| A car or guardrail pass counts when it is | Per hour in dark or icy hours | App's choice with a network retrained on that definition (389 hours) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 cm or closer (current) | 1.26 | 40 km/h in every hour |
| closer than 20 cm | 0.99 | 70 km/h in 313 hours, 60 in 29, 50 in 12, 40 in 35 |
| 15 cm or closer | 0.64 | 70 km/h in every hour |
| 10 cm or closer | 0.26 | 70 km/h in every hour |

Changing "20 cm or closer" to "closer than 20 cm" alone flips the app from 40 to mostly 70 km/h. The network doesn't really decide; the definition does. (Thanks to Cristian's check, which spotted the "or closer".)

## What would and wouldn't help

- **Better models or more features:** no. Tried above, all land on the average.
- **Synthetic data:** no. Data copied from ours repeats the same lack of a pattern. Data made from invented rules teaches the network our rules, not reality. It is useful for one thing: testing that the pipeline can learn a known relationship.
- **More real data:** yes. Several months, accident locations, traffic per hour.

## Comparison with the IMS case study

- IMS's network was also poor (test error 147 to 155 cm for water levels around 175 cm) and was never improved. The deck calls it "First Success" once it runs.
- IMS's training data was generated by an LLM (slide 112), with the relationship described in the prompt.
- IMS states its key assumption explicitly (slide 18: "There is a connection between the sensor readings and the water level"). Our matching assumption, that the conditions and the limit affect near-accidents, is contradicted by our data.
- IMS's REQ2 ("must not be too optimistic in 99.9%") justifies being cautious when unsure, like our 40 km/h fallback.
